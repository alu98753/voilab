Overview
This semester, the course project focuses on building an intelligent robotic
manipulation system using the UMI device [1] and the Isaac Sim simulator [2]. You
will first collect real-world demonstration data and then reconstruct these
demonstrations in simulation to create training datasets. Next, you will train a
manipulation policy and evaluate its performance across different simulated
environment layouts. The goal is to develop a policy that can reliably perform
pick-and-place operations in the simulated ED305 scene, even under varying object
configurations. Through this project, you will gain hands-on experience with the
complete robot learning pipeline, from data collection, training, and deployment.

Pipeline
STEP1. Real-World Data Collection with UMI
Using the UMI [1] devices that you assembled during the course, collect real-world
demonstration data in the ED305 classroom. Each group is assigned one of the
following three pick-and-place tasks:
●​ Cup Stacking in the Kitchen: Randomly place the blue and pink cups upside
down on the countertop. Then, pick up the blue cup and stack it on top of the
pink cup.
●​ Cutlery Arrangement on the Dining Table: Randomly place the fork and
knife on the table. Then, place the knife on the right side of the plate and the
fork on the left side of the plate.
●​ Toy Blocks Collection in the Living Room: Randomly place three toy
blocks on the tabletop. Then, collect all the scattered blocks and place them
into the white basket.

1

STEP2. Simulation Reconstruction and Data Re-Collection
Since direct operation of a real robotic arm in the ED305 classroom is currently not
available, we provide a corresponding virtual environment in NVIDIA Isaac Sim [2].
In this step, you will run the scripts we provide to reproduce real-world
demonstrations within the simulation. This process automatically collects the
corresponding observations and robot end-effector trajectories in the virtual
environment, which will serve as the training data for subsequent model learning.

Due to some limitations encountered during implementation, the reproduced
demonstrations in simulation only preserve the object poses from the original
recordings, rather than the full robot motion trajectories. Instead, we employ a
naive rule-based motion planning method to generate feasible actions for task
execution. In addition, due to unexpected issues that prevent the robotic arm from
reliably grasping objects, we adopt a simplified grasping approximation, where
objects are “attached” to the robot end-effector and move together with it. It is also
worth noting that certain object properties (e.g., object size and geometry) and
environment configurations in simulation may not perfectly match those in the real
world. These discrepancies are introduced as practical engineering adjustments to
ensure the overall system remains functional and the data collection pipeline can
operate reliably. You may also observe that the reconstructed object poses
occasionally differ from those obtained in your own collected data. Such

2

discrepancies can arise from detection and coordinate transformation errors, as well
as physical interactions and collisions within the simulated environment.
Nevertheless, this data collection pipeline is designed to record a demonstration only
when the task success criteria are satisfied, ensuring the overall validity of the
collected dataset.

STEP3. Model Training with Diffusion Policy
After collecting the simulated dataset, the next step is to train a model to perform the
corresponding pick-and-place tasks. We adopt the Diffusion Policy [3], a recently
popular imitation learning approach in robotic learning. Given the environment
observations and the robot state, the model learns to generate the corresponding
robot end-effector trajectories. In this stage, the collected dataset is split into training
and validation sets, and evaluates the training performance in an offline manner.

STEP4. Inference and Evaluation
During inference, the trained policy is deployed in the simulation and evaluated
online under different environment layouts to assess its capability, with a particular
focus on robustness to varying object placements. The scripts used for this
evaluation will not be released. Instead, we will perform the evaluation using
the trained diffusion policy model weights submitted by each group. Model
performance will be ranked separately for each task. The group achieving the best
performance on each task will receive additional bonus points on the final total
course score.

Final Notes & Tips
We encourage you to explore, experiment, and have fun throughout this project. This
pipeline is designed to help you gain hands-on experience with the full robotic
learning workflow, from data collection to model training and evaluation. Before you
start, here are two important tips to keep in mind:
●​ Data quality matters. The performance of your model largely depends on the
data you collect. Think carefully about the final task requirements and
evaluation settings, and design your data accordingly.
○​ Although collecting additional real-world data is not allowed, you can
modify the data generation scripts to obtain more training data. Just
make sure to clearly describe any changes you make in both the
presentation and the report. Possible aspects you can adjust include,
but are not limited to, the following:
■​ Solve real-world UMI data processing issue: As you may
have experienced previously, UMI data conversion is not always
guaranteed to succeed for every recording. You are expected to
3

analyze the data processing pipeline and identify potential
reasons that could cause these conversion failures, and if there
is a possibility to resolve them, because the simulation data
collection is highly dependent on real-world collected data.
■​ Improve simulation data collection: You may modify the
predefined pipeline in the simulation data collection stage, for
example, (1) improve pick-and-place strategy by introducing
multiple grasp poses or adding randomized motion to the robot
trajectory, (2) change camera pose (default is eye-in-hand) to
have better view of environments.
■​ Build up custom data collection methods: You may
implement your own data collection interfaces or pipelines in
simulation to generate additional training data. For example, you
may: (1) introduce keyboard-based control for manual data
collection, or (2) design a retry mechanism that allows multiple
attempts within each episode by applying different modifications
until data is successfully collected.
●​ Hyperparameters are crucial. Model performance is highly sensitive to
hyperparameter choices. In the beginning, you can use the default
parameters for training, but we strongly recommend referring to the original
Diffusion Policy [3] paper for detailed discussions and insights. You will likely
gain valuable understanding from it.
○​ In particular, some hyperparameters are closely related to task horizon
length and multi-step behaviors, so we encourage you to experiment
with different parameter combinations.
